The Metrics Ladder

ExamTriple Shot

Written by Claude for Anna, Nov 10 2026, after task 2.3 went 0 for 3. Standard ML definitions; the AWS-specific names are from the SageMaker XGBoost metrics page and the Clarify bias pages linked at the bottom. Not AWS material.

The ladder: four questions, in this order, every time

  1. Numbers or labels?Predicting a number (price, demand, temperature) is regression: RMSE, MAE, R². Predicting a label (fraud or not, cat or dog) is classification: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC. Cross the two and you are wrong before you start.
  2. Any mansions?A few huge values or huge errors. Regression: MAE, not RMSE. Filling gaps: median, not mean.
  3. Is there a threshold yet?No cutoff chosen, or comparing how well models rank? AUC. It is the only one that does not need a threshold.
  4. Is the needle rare?Rare positive class: never accuracy. Costly miss: recall. Costly false alarm: precision. Both: F1.
Say it: Numbers or labels. Mansions. Threshold. Needle.

1Numbers or labels?

Every metric question hides this test in the first sentence. "Price in dollars", "demand next week", "how many minutes" are numbers. "Is it fraud", "which category", "will they churn" are labels. The option list always contains metrics from the other family as traps.

Question #403 asked for the error of a house-price model and you picked AUC. Price is a number, so AUC and accuracy were both out before reading the rest. Two of four options gone in one second.

2Any mansions?

MAE averages the errors as they are. RMSE squares them first, then square-roots the average. Squaring makes a big miss count enormously more than a small one.

Five houses. Four are off by $10k, one mansion is off by $1M.
MAE = (10k + 10k + 10k + 10k + 1M) / 5 = $208k.
RMSE = √((10k² × 4 + 1M²) / 5) ≈ $447k.
The same five errors, and the mansion more than doubles the number. In MAE the $1M miss counts 100× a $10k miss. In RMSE it counts 10,000×.

So: "typical error", "don't let a few outliers dominate" means MAE. "Large errors are much worse than small ones", "penalize big misses" means RMSE. Neither is always right; the stem tells you which fear the business has.

The same mansion logic runs the mean versus median choice when filling missing values.

Ten incomes: nine people earn $50k, one earns $10M.
Mean = (9 × 50k + 10M) / 10 = $1,045,000. Median = $50k.
Filling the 8% missing incomes with the mean gives everyone a millionaire's salary. The median stays at a normal person.
Say it: Mansions break the mean and square badly. MAE and median ignore them.

3Is there a threshold yet?

A classifier does not output "fraud". It outputs a score, say 0.83. Someone must pick a cutoff: above 0.5 is fraud, or above 0.9, or above 0.2. Accuracy, precision, recall and F1 all change when that cutoff moves. They are threshold metrics.

AUC is different. Pick one random fraud case and one random legitimate case. AUC is the chance the fraud case got the higher score. That question never mentions a cutoff, so AUC works across all of them at once.

0.50 is a coin flip: the model ranks fraud above legit half the time, which is no ranking at all.
0.88 means that in 88 of 100 random pairs the fraud case scores higher.
1.00 is perfect ranking. Nothing to do with "88% accuracy".
Question #436 said "the business hasn't chosen a decision threshold yet" and you picked "nothing can be concluded". That line was the hint, not the obstacle. No threshold yet is exactly when AUC is the metric to use.

Cues: "rank", "no threshold chosen", "compare models before deployment", "across operating points" mean AUC (ROC AUC, or PR AUC when the positive class is rare).

Say it: AUC is a coin at 0.50 and needs no cutoff.

4Is the needle rare?

One thousand transactions, ten are fraud. Two models:

Model A: "never fraud"Model B: flags 48, catches 8
Caught fraud (TP)08
Missed fraud (FN)102
False alarms (FP)040
Accuracy99.0%96.0%
Recall (of the real fraud, how much did I catch?)0%80%
Precision (of what I flagged, how much was real?)undefined17%

Model A has the higher accuracy and is worthless. That is the accuracy trap: with a rare class, a model that never says yes scores 99%. Accuracy is the wrong metric whenever the stem says imbalanced, rare, 1%, or fraud.

Then pick which mistake is expensive:

  • Missing a real one is expensive (cancer, fraud, a failing machine): you want recall. Recall = Real ones Retrieved.
  • A false alarm is expensive (blocking a real customer, waking an engineer at 3 a.m., spam filter eating a job offer): you want precision. Precision = my Picks are Pure.
  • Both hurt: F1, the balance of the two. One number, but it still needs a threshold.

Moving the threshold trades one for the other. Lower the cutoff and you catch more (recall up) but flag more innocents (precision down). The exam loves "the team lowered the threshold; what happens to recall?" The answer is recall up, precision down.

Say it: Rare means never accuracy. Costly miss, recall. Costly alarm, precision.
